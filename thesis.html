Bachelor’s thesis

The macroeconomic impact of artificial intelligence

Productivity, employment and the European outlook. Firms report real gains from AI, yet national productivity data shows almost nothing. The thesis asks whether AI is already a driver of growth, or whether its effects will be smaller, slower and more uneven than the public debate suggests.

01 / 06

The question

Since generative AI went public in late 2022, banks and consultancies have forecast growth comparable to steam or electricity. Goldman Sachs put the gain at +7% of world GDP.

Productivity in advanced economies has stayed weak. The thesis moves from theory, to the empirical evidence, to the European case, and separates what the technology can do from what is actually measurable in growth.

02 / 06

Two models, one answer

Acemoglu (2024) splits production into tasks and adds them up with Hulten’s theorem: the productivity gain equals the share of tasks AI affects times the cost it saves on them. Only 4.6% of tasks are profitable to automate within ten years, so the total gain is small.

Brynjolfsson et al. (2021) explain the timing: firms first invest in intangibles that statistics do not record, such as training, reorganisation and data. Measured productivity first dips, then rises: the productivity J-curve.

03 / 06

Inside firms and jobs

At firm level the gains are real but uneven. They go to firms with in-house IT skills, data and the capacity to reorganise. Firms that buy AI without those skills see more volatile productivity, not higher.

For workers the picture is double. Within a job, AI spreads the best practices to less experienced staff. Across the labour market, demand shifts toward highly skilled profiles.

04 / 06

Why it is not in the data yet

Total factor productivity shows no acceleration linked to AI. Both models predicted this: modest gains, and late. Applied to 31 European countries, Acemoglu’s method gives about 1% over five years.

Two costs are already visible: employment falls in the US areas most exposed to AI, and data centres raise electricity use and emissions. They use about 1.5% of world electricity today, 3% by 2030 (IEA).

05 / 06

Europe

Adoption is accelerating: one EU firm in five used AI in 2025. Most are buyers of ready-made tools; few develop their own, and those are where the larger returns sit.

The gap with the US is not access to the technology but the capital around it: skills, venture capital, computing infrastructure and market scale. The Draghi report puts the investment need at €750–800bn a year.

Risks and conclusion

Three open risks for Europe: dependence on US firms along the AI value chain, electricity two to three times more expensive than in the US, and an ageing workforce, where AI may matter more for growth than as a threat to jobs.

The conclusion: the distance between AI’s promise and its footprint on productivity is not a failure of the technology. It is the time it takes to build the skills and organisation that make it productive, and that is where policy can act.
